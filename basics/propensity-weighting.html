Why weighting?

Different people end up in different groups.
Before comparing outcomes, let each group represent the same population.

Three cities. 280 people. We want their average income.

A bigger city contributes more people, so it should have more influence.

Each city now supplies one third of the sample. The smallest city has too much influence; the largest has too little.

Give every sampled person equal weight14.6

The sample means are 9.5, 14.6 and 19.6. Averaging them equally gives 14.57.

Give the cities their population shares16.9

Now the same observations represent 30, 90 and 160 people. The weighted average is 16.91.

Show the arithmetic

Sampling chances: 10/30, 10/90 and 10/160. Inverse chances: 3, 9 and 16.

9.5 × 30/280 + 14.6 × 90/280 + 19.6 × 160/280 = 16.91

The rounded population means imply 17.46. Weighting gets closer here, but the small samples still differ from their cities. It does not promise an exact answer in every sample.

The idea to keep: weighting changes who the sample represents.

Now use that idea for treatment →
02
Notice selection

How did people get into each group?

Severity affects treatment and outcome. If the treated group contains more high-severity people, its outcome reflects both differences.

Name the chance

Propensity score

The chance of treatment, given a person's pretreatment covariates.

g(X) = P(A = 1 | X)
Decode the notation and choose the covariates
A
Treatment: 1 for treated, 0 for untreated.
X
Pretreatment covariates used for adjustment, such as severity.
g(X)
A treatment probability. You may also see it written e(L).

We usually estimate it with a treatment model, such as logistic regression. It predicts treatment, not the outcome. Choose adjustment variables using causal knowledge: adding a mediator or collider can create a different problem.

Turn a chance into a weight

Use the probability of the treatment actually received. An unusual treatment history gets more relative weight.

Treated personReveal the weight →
1 / g(X)

If g(X) = 0.25:
1 / 0.25 = 4

Untreated personReveal the weight →
1 / (1 − g(X))

If g(X) = 0.25:
1 / 0.75 ≈ 1.33

These unstabilized weights are at least 1. “Downweight” means less relative influence; it need not mean a raw weight below 1.

Follow the people and the calculation

Without treatment, mean outcomes are 2 at low severity and 5 at high severity. Treatment adds 2. Changing assignment leaves those outcomes fixed.

At the start, the treated group has 10 low-severity and 40 high-severity people in expectation. Weights 5 and 1.25 turn those totals into 50 and 50. The untreated group returns to the same target mix.

Exact expected frequencies, not a random sample of real patients. The contrast uses normalized weighted means within each arm. Weighted totals are representation; effective sample size measures weight concentration. Neither is a count of new independent patients.

Why does the treatment mechanism cancel?
f(X) × f(A | X) × f(Y | A, X)

Dividing by the correct received-treatment probability removes f(A | X). After normalization, both arms have the same f(X). Outcomes within each (A, X) cell stay the same.

Stabilized weights use P(A = a) / P(A = a | X). They retain the overall treatment proportions in this population calculation. A constant multiplier within an arm leaves its normalized mean and effective sample size unchanged.

Standardization instead compares outcomes within covariate groups and averages over the target mix. The two routes express the same identified population target; fitted estimates in finite samples can differ.

Now in R: estimate the score, weight, and check balance

Download the base R example. It fits a treatment model and reproduces the starting table, with no packages or external data.

ps <- glm(A ~ X, family = binomial())
g <- predict(ps, type = "response")
w <- ifelse(A == 1, 1/g, 1/(1-g))
ipw <- weighted.mean(Y[A == 1], w[A == 1]) -
       weighted.mean(Y[A == 0], w[A == 0])

Observed difference: 3.80. Weighted difference: 2.00. High-severity shares: 0.80/0.20 before, 0.50/0.50 after. This demonstrates point estimation and balance; it does not compute uncertainty with estimated weights.

Check what the weights can and cannot fix →
04
Check before you trust

Did the weights do their job?

Look at balance, overlap and assumptions. Predicting treatment well is not the same as building a useful comparison.

Check balanceDo the measured covariates line up?

Compare covariates before and after weighting. Inspect distributions as well as means.

Here, adjustment brings the differences closer to zero. A fitted model will not necessarily balance a finite sample.

A standardized mean difference divides the group-mean difference by a reference standard deviation. Keep that reference fixed before and after adjustment. |SMD| < 0.1 is a common benchmark, not a certificate of causality. A balanced propensity score alone does not establish covariate balance.

Check supportWho is carrying the evidence?
A rare history

A treated person with g(X) = 0.02 has unstabilized weight 50. A few people may carry much of the comparison.

A missing history

If a target stratum has no chance of one treatment, there is nobody to upweight. That is a positivity failure.

Try the stress cases above. Watch the effective sample sizes fall as weight concentration increases.

Effective sample size = (sum of weights)² / sum of squared weights

Truncating large weights can change balance and bias. It cannot create missing treatment support.

Another use of the score: matching

Matching selects or weights comparable sets. Matching centered on treated people commonly targets the ATT; dropping treated people can further change the target. Choose the target first.

Check the assumptionsWhat lets the contrast be causal?
Consistency
Treatment is well defined, and observed outcomes correspond to the treatment received.
Conditional exchangeability
The adjustment covariates suffice to remove confounding.
Positivity
Each treatment needed by the target has positive probability at each relevant covariate value.

IPW estimation also needs sufficiently accurate treatment probabilities and statistical regularity. “As if randomized” describes the intended measured balance; weighting does not randomize the study.

Keep the boundary clear: measured balance cannot tell us whether an important confounder was left unmeasured.

Carry the idea forward

Keep the weights. Change what they correct.

The same treatment probabilities appear again as our estimators develop.

IPW

Weight outcomes

Let each treatment group represent the target population.

AIPW

Weight prediction errors

Predict the outcomes first, then correct using weighted residuals.

TMLE

Target the fit

Use the probabilities to guide an update to the outcome model.

Build AIPWPredictions plus weighted corrections

A residual is an observed outcome minus its prediction. AIPW combines a predicted contrast with two residual corrections.

1. Predict a contrastm̂₁(X) − m̂₀(X)
Add the weighted corrections
2. Correct treated predictions+ A / ĝ(X) × {Y − m̂₁(X)}
3. Correct untreated predictions− (1 − A) / (1 − ĝ(X)) × {Y − m̂₀(X)}

Average the resulting contributions. If the outcome model is right, residual corrections average to zero. If the propensity model is right, the corrections can repair a wrong outcome model.

See double robustness in the teaching world

Exact population calculations for the two-stratum experiment. Omitting severity uses outcome predictions 3 and 4. Propensity 0.5 is wrong at the start; it is correct if both treatment chances are 0.5. This illustrates consistency, not efficiency or a confidence-interval guarantee.

Double robustness: either nuisance model can be correct. The causal assumptions still apply, and inference needs additional conditions.
