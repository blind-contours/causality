Averages depend on the mix

One source of the problem
Imbalanced groups. Treated and untreated patients differ in who they include, so comparing them directly can mislead.
Intuitive remedy
Rebalance the groups so both look like the same population.

Rebalancing works because an average depends on the mix of people behind it. Without the new treatment, 20% of low-severity patients in our hospital die and 50% of high-severity patients die. The hospital's death rate is a weighted averageMultiply each value by its weight and add; the weights sum to 1. of those two rates, with each group weighted by its share of patients. Move the slider to change that share.

01

Comparing groups at the same mix

Now a new treatment arrives. Half of the 100 patients have high severity, and doctors give the treatment mostly to them: 80% of high-severity patients are treated, compared with 20% of low-severity patients. At first glance the treatment looks harmful.

Suppose both groups had the hospital's mix, half low and half high severity. Which would have the lower death rate?

This is direct standardizationDescribed by the actuary Neison in 1844, who used it to compare death rates across districts with different age structures.: apply each group's severity-specific rates to a shared standard populationThe mix every group is reweighted to. Using the whole hospital as the standard compares treating everyone with treating no one., here the hospital itself. Severity is a confounderA common cause of treatment and outcome. Sicker patients are more likely to be treated and more likely to die. because it affects both who is treated and who dies, and the reversal is an example of Simpson's paradoxA comparison that reverses direction when groups with different mixes are combined..

02

Two potential outcomes for every patient

Standardization answers a what-if question: what would the death rate be if all 100 patients were treated, and what would it be if none were? The difference between those two rates is the average treatment effectATE = E[Y(1)] − E[Y(0)]: the mean outcome if everyone were treated minus the mean outcome if no one were..

In the potential outcomesThe framework goes back to Neyman (1923) for experiments and Rubin (1974) for observational studies. framework, each patient has an outcome under treatment, Y(1), and an outcome without it, Y(0). Picture World 1, where everyone is treated, and World 0, where no one is. We see each patient in only one of them.

The treated patients are therefore a skewed sample of World 1. As the bars above show, 40 of the 50 have high severity, compared with half of the hospital. Each patient's chance of being in that sample is their propensity score.

Propensity score

g(x) = P(A = 1 | X = x)

The probability of treatment given covariates x (Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983If treatment is unconfounded given X, it is also unconfounded given g(X) alone, so one number can carry the adjustment.). Here g = 0.2 for low severity and 0.8 for high severity. In real data g is estimated, often with logistic regression.

03

Inverse probability weighting

Survey statisticians handle unequal sampling chances by weighting each sampled person by one over their chance of selection (Horvitz and Thompson, 1952With known, positive selection probabilities, the weighted sum is unbiased for the population total.). A person sampled with probability 1/5 represents 5 people. Propensity score weighting applies the same rule in each world, using the probability of the treatment each patient actually received.

Treated patient

weight = 1 / g(x)

Untreated patient

weight = 1 / (1 − g(x))

Weights in the hospital
TreatedUntreated
Low · g = 0.21 / 0.2 = 51 / 0.8 = 1.25
High · g = 0.81 / 0.8 = 1.251 / 0.2 = 5

04

What the weights rely on

  1. 1

    ExchangeabilityY(a) is independent of A given X, for each a. The data cannot confirm it; it comes from knowing how treatment was decided.

    Severity accounts for every shared reason behind treatment and death. If doctors also noticed frailty that nobody recorded, weights based on severity would leave both worlds skewed.

  2. 2

    Positivity0 < g(x) < 1 for every x that occurs in the target population.

    Every kind of patient had some chance of each treatment.

  3. 3

    ConsistencyIf A = a, then Y = Y(a). It assumes one well-defined version of each treatment and no interference between patients.

    Treatment means the same thing for everyone, and one patient's treatment does not affect another's outcome.

Rare treatments cause a milder version of the same problem. If 2% of a group is treated, each treated patient gets weight 50 and the estimate rests on a few people. The lab shows this.

Appendix

Why weighting identifies E[Y(1)]

For World 1, under the three assumptions:

  1. E[ A·Y / g(X) ] = E[ A·Y(1) / g(X) ]consistency
  2. = E[ E[A | X] · E[Y(1) | X] / g(X) ]iterated expectation, then exchangeability: Y(1) ⫫ A | X
  3. = E[ E[Y(1) | X] ] = E[Y(1)]positivity: g(X) > 0, and E[A | X] = g(X)

The untreated world is the same with 1 − A and 1 − g(X). Standardization targets the same quantity through the g-formula:

E[Y(1)] = Σx P(X = x) · E[Y | A = 1, X = x]

In the hospital

StandardizationWeighting
World 10.5 × 10% + 0.5 × 40% = 25%(1 × 5 + 16 × 1.25) / 100 = 25%
World 00.5 × 20% + 0.5 × 50% = 35%(8 × 1.25 + 5 × 5) / 100 = 35%

The two agree exactly here because the scores are computed within the same severity groups that standardization uses. With fitted models in real data they usually differ a little.

Horvitz–Thompson and Hájek

Horvitz–Thompson divides the weighted deaths by everyone, n = 100. The normalized (Hájek) version divides by the sum of the weights in each arm. Here both sums are exactly 100, so they agree. In real samples they differ, and the normalized version is usually steadier. The lab reports normalized means.

Stabilized weights

Multiply by the overall share receiving that treatment: P(A = a) / P(A = a | X). Within each arm the normalized mean and effective sample size do not change, and weight totals become roughly the group sizes. Stabilization mainly matters for marginal structural models that are not saturated, such as those for treatments over time, where it reduces variance.

A missing-data problem

Keiding and Clayton (2014) note that standardizing to the whole population can be read as an inverse probability weighting method, where the weighting compensates for not observing each person's other exposure state.

Diagnostics

In real data the propensity score is estimated, so check what the weights achieved rather than trusting the model that made them.

Balance. Compare each measured covariate between arms before and after weighting. A standardized mean difference divides the gap by a reference standard deviation; keep that reference fixed before and after. |SMD| < 0.1 is a common benchmark. Look at distributions, not just means.

Overlap. Plot the propensity scores by arm. Where one arm is missing, weighting has nothing to work with.

Weight concentration. ESS = (Σ w)² / Σ w², the effective sample size. When a few weights dominate, the ESS falls even though the weighted mix looks perfect.

Trimming. Truncating extreme weights trades bias for variance. It cannot create missing support, and it can change the population you are describing.

Balance on measured covariates says nothing about unmeasured ones.

Augmented IPW

AIPW starts from an outcome model's prediction of both worlds and uses the weights only on the prediction errors:

  1. m̂₁(X) − m̂₀(X)predicted difference for each patient
  2. + A / ĝ(X) · {Y − m̂₁(X)}fix World 1 with weighted residuals
  3. − (1 − A) / (1 − ĝ(X)) · {Y − m̂₀(X)}fix World 0 the same way

Average over patients. If the outcome model is right, the residual corrections average to zero. If the propensity model is right, they repair a wrong outcome model. This property is called double robustness.

Exact population values in the hospital, using the lab's current settings. The wrong outcome model ignores severity and predicts each arm's crude death rate. Double robustness concerns consistency only. It does not guarantee efficiency or valid intervals, and it does not address unmeasured confounding.

The hospital in base R

100 rows, one per patient. Fit the propensity model, weight, and compare with standardization. Download the script.

n <- c(10, 40, 40, 10); deaths <- c(1, 8, 16, 5)
d <- data.frame(X = rep(c(0, 0, 1, 1), n), A = rep(c(1, 0, 1, 0), n),
                Y = unlist(mapply(function(k, m) rep(1:0, c(k, m - k)), deaths, n)))
ps <- glm(A ~ X, data = d, family = binomial())
g  <- predict(ps, type = "response")
w  <- ifelse(d$A == 1, 1 / g, 1 / (1 - g))
weighted.mean(d$Y[d$A == 1], w[d$A == 1])   # 0.25, World 1
weighted.mean(d$Y[d$A == 0], w[d$A == 0])   # 0.35, World 0

Point estimates and balance only. For uncertainty with estimated weights, see the standard-errors lesson. In practice, WeightIt::weightit() estimates weights and cobalt::bal.tab() checks balance.

The same idea with three cities

Three cities hold 30, 90 and 160 people, with mean incomes of about 10.1, 15.4 and 20.0. The whole population's mean is about 17.5.

Sample 10 people from each city and average them equally: 14.6. The small city gets as much say as the big one. Now weight each sampled person by one over their chance of being sampled: 30/10 = 3, 90/10 = 9 and 160/10 = 16 people. The weighted mean is 16.9, much closer to 17.5. The remaining gap is ordinary sampling noise.
