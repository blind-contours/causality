A short bridge between two pictures

One Move, Two Faces

A short bridge lesson. You have seen two pictures of "moving the fit". In the scores lessons you tilted a density to read off a score. In Two Strata, One Step and Where the Clever Covariate Comes From you shifted a regression line. Here is why those are the same move.

Multiply a bell curve of Y by a factor that grows with y, then renormalize so it is a density again. What happens?

It slides toward larger y, and for a bell curve it keeps its shape. That slide of the curve's centre is exactly a shift of the fitted outcome, the regression line on the right. Scene 1 works this out, and Scene 2 shows one more object that has been hiding: the sample itself.

Scene 1

Tilt the density of Y at one x, and the regression line is what moves

This step's world two severity strata, drawn as Gaussian curves with variance 1 · outcome Y · the true treated means m₁(high) = 3, m₁(low) = 2, the same numbers as the sharper version of the course cohort in Two Strata, One Step

Fix a patient's severity x and look at the density of Y among treated patients, p̂(y | 1, x). The initial fit says it is Normal with variance 1 and mean m̂₁(x), the fitted treated-outcome regression. Now multiply it by exp(ε·H·(y − m̂₁)) and renormalize.

Two things happen at once. The score of this path at ε = 0 is d/dε log p̂ε = H·(y − m̂₁), the outcome piece of the influence function. And a Gaussian times an exponential in y is a Gaussian: the tilted density is Normal with mean m̂₁ + εH (with variance σ² the new mean is m̂₁ + εHσ², and the variance does not change).

That is the regression fluctuation you used in Where the Clever Covariate Comes From, derived rather than declared. H = 1/g(x), where g(x) is the propensity score, the chance of treatment at severity x. Both strata use the same ε.

p̂_ε(y | 1, x) ∝ p̂(y | 1, x) · exp(ε H(x) (y − m̂₁(x))) score at ε = 0: H(x)(y − m̂₁(x))
Gaussian case: exp(−(y−m̂₁)²/2) · exp(εH(y−m̂₁)) ∝ exp(−(y − m̂₁ − εH)²/2) ⇒ mean = m̂₁ + εH
Treatment is common at high severity, g(high) = 0.9, and rare at low severity, g(low) = 0.1. With the same ε, how far does the low-severity mean move compared with the high-severity mean?
The mean of the tilted density is computed by numerical integration over a wide window (both means ± 12 SD), not from the formula, so the readout is a check that the algebra is right. The score panels share one vertical scale, so the steeper low-severity line is really steeper.

The ratio of the two shifts is H(low)/H(high) = g(high)/g(low) = 0.9/0.1 = 9. It is the same 9 as the budget ray in Two Strata, One Step, for the same reason.

In one sentenceThe TMLE fluctuation of the outcome regression is a one-parameter exponential tilt of the conditional density of Y along the outcome component of the efficient influence function. For a Gaussian working model that tilt is a location shift by εH, which is why the update looks like a regression with H as the covariate.
Scene 2

Pₙ is a point too, and the correction is a gap between two averages

This step's world one measurement Z per person, drawn from a two-bump Gaussian mixture; n = 40 by default · target: the mean of Z · compared with its exact true mean μ

Three probability distributions can all average a function. P is the truth, a smooth density with mean μ. P̂ is the fit, smooth but with the wrong mean μ̂. Pₙ is the sample, a spike of mass 1/n at each observation; it is not a smooth density, but it averages functions just the same.

For a mean, the influence function at the fit is ϕ̂(z) = z − μ̂. Average it under each of the three.

What is the average of ϕ̂(z) = z − μ̂ under the fitted distribution P̂?

Under P̂ the average is zero by construction: the fit is centered on itself. Under Pₙ it is z̄ − μ̂, the one-step correction. Under P it is μ − μ̂, the negative of the plug-in bias.

So the correction is (Pₙ − P̂)ϕ̂, and it estimates (P − P̂)ϕ̂ with sampling error (Pₙ − P)ϕ̂ that shrinks like 1/√n. That last term is what a standard error measures.

P̂ ϕ̂ = 0 Pₙ ϕ̂ = z̄ − μ̂ (correction) P ϕ̂ = μ − μ̂ (− plug-in bias)
one-step = ψ(P̂) + Pₙ ϕ̂ = μ̂ + (z̄ − μ̂) = z̄ error = (Pₙ − P) ϕ̂ = Oₚ(1/√n)
Press New sample a few times and watch Pₙϕ̂ jump around P ϕ̂. That jitter is (Pₙ − P)ϕ̂, and its size is what the standard error reports.
In one sentenceThe one-step correction Pₙϕ̂ is the empirical average of the influence function at the fit, ϕ̂ = ϕ(P̂). Because P̂ϕ̂ = 0, it equals (Pₙ − P̂)ϕ̂: the sample’s disagreement with the fit, measured in the direction that matters for ψ. It estimates the negative of the plug-in bias, with sampling noise of order 1/√n.

What is exact here: the tilted mean in Scene 1 is a numerical integral that matches m̂₁ + εH to many decimals. What is simulated: the sample in Scene 2 (press New sample). What is schematic: the Gaussian working model with variance 1, and the two strata of Scene 1.
