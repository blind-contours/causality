The bridge between units 2–4 and units 6–7

One Move, Two Faces

Units 2 to 4 moved a density: pε = p(1 + εh). Units 6 and 7 will move a regression: με = μ̂ + εH. Those look like different operations on different objects. They are one move. Tilting the conditional density of Y by a factor that grows with the residual has score H·(y − μ̂), and its mean is μ̂ + εH. This page shows that, then shows the other object that has been hiding: the empirical distribution Pₙ is a point in the same space as P and P̂, and the one-step correction is the gap between averaging D under Pₙ and under P̂.

Scene 1

Tilt the density of Y at one x, and the regression line is what moves

Fix a patient's covariate x and look at the conditional density of Y given treatment, p̂(y | 1, x). The initial fit says it is Normal with mean μ̂(x) and variance 1. Now multiply it by exp(ε·H·(y − μ̂)) and renormalize. Two things happen at once. The score of this path at ε = 0 is d/dε log p̂ε = H·(y − μ̂), the outcome piece of the influence function. And because a Gaussian times an exponential in y is a Gaussian, the tilted density is Normal with mean μ̂ + εH: the regression fluctuation from unit 7, derived rather than declared. Both strata use the same ε; H differs, so the old curve moves 9× as far.

p̂_ε(y | 1, x) ∝ p̂(y | 1, x) · exp(ε H(x) (y − μ̂(x))) score at ε = 0: H(x)(y − μ̂(x))
Gaussian case: exp(−(y−μ̂)²/2) · exp(εH(y−μ̂)) ∝ exp(−(y − μ̂ − εH)²/2) ⇒ mean = μ̂ + εH
With one shared ε, how far does the old stratum’s density mean move compared with the young stratum’s?
The mean of the tilted density is computed by numerical integration, not from the formula, so the readout is a check that the algebra is right.
In one sentenceThe TMLE fluctuation of the outcome regression is a one-parameter exponential tilt of the conditional density of Y along the outcome component of the efficient influence function. For a Gaussian working model that tilt is a location shift by εH, which is why the update looks like a regression with H as the covariate.
Scene 2

Pₙ is a point too, and the correction is a gap between two averages

Three distributions live in the same space. P is the truth, a smooth density. P̂ is the fit, smooth but with the wrong mean. Pₙ is the sample, a spike of mass 1/n at each observation. Average the same function D̂(z) = z − μ̂ under each. Under P̂ the average is zero by construction: the fit is centered on itself. Under Pₙ it is z̄ − μ̂, the one-step correction. Under P it is μ − μ̂, the negative of the plug-in bias. So the correction is (Pₙ − P̂)D̂, and it estimates (P − P̂)D̂ with sampling error (Pₙ − P)D̂ that shrinks like 1/√n. That last term is the one the influence-function chapter is about.

P̂ D̂ = 0 Pₙ D̂ = z̄ − μ̂ (correction) P D̂ = μ − μ̂ (− plug-in bias)
one-step = ψ(P̂) + Pₙ D̂ = μ̂ + (z̄ − μ̂) = z̄ error = (Pₙ − P) D̂ = Oₚ(1/√n)
What is the average of D̂(z) = z − μ̂ under the fitted distribution P̂?
Press New sample a few times and watch PₙD̂ jump around P D̂. That jitter is (Pₙ − P)D̂, and its size is what the standard error reports.
In one sentenceThe one-step correction PₙD(P̂) is the empirical average of the influence function at the fit. Because P̂D(P̂) = 0, it equals (Pₙ − P̂)D(P̂): the sample’s disagreement with the fit, measured in the direction that matters for ψ. It is an estimate of the plug-in bias with 1/√n noise.
