Semiparametric efficiency, drawn

The one-step estimator, as a shape

Three acts. First the geometry: a plug-in is a point on a curve, the influence function is the tangent, and one Newton step lands you within the curvature of the truth. Then the same thing on simulated data, one operation at a time. Then 300 repeats, so you can see the bias go and the width stay.

Act 1

The curve ψ(Pε) along the path from your estimate to the truth

Put the fitted distribution P̂ at ε = 0 and the true P at ε = 1, and walk the straight line between them, Pε = P̂ + ε(P − P̂). The parameter traced along that walk is a curve. Its value at the left end is the plug-in; at the right end, the truth. The tangent at the left end has slope P D(P̂) = ∫ D(P̂) d(P − P̂), the pathwise derivative in the direction of the truth; the plug-in's first-order bias is its negative. Extending the tangent to ε = 1 is the one-step estimator. What the tangent misses is the curvature: the second-order remainder R2.

ψ(P̂) − ψ(P) = −P D(P̂) + R2(P̂, P) ⇒ ψone-step = ψ(P̂) + Pn D(P̂) ≈ ψ(P) + R2 + (Pn − P) D
plug-in ψ(P̂) tangent → one-step remainder R2 truth ψ(P)
Drag δ down and watch: the plug-in bias (first order) shrinks like δ, the remainder shrinks like δ². That gap between "like δ" and "like δ²" is the whole reason nuisances at n−1/4 give a target at n−1/2.
Act 2

One step on data: g-computation, then the correction

Simulated cohort. Treatment is more likely for higher X, the outcome is nonlinear in X, and the true average treatment effect is exactly 2. The outcome model is an S-learner: one linear regression of Y on (X, A). The slider shrinks its treatment coefficient toward zero, the way a regularized learner would. The propensity model can be made anywhere from correct to useless. Step through and watch where the correction comes from: it is the inverse-propensity-weighted average of the outcome model's own residuals.

ψ̂one-step = Pn[ μ̂1(X) − μ̂0(X) ] + Pn[ A/π̂(X) · (Y − μ̂1(X)) − (1−A)/(1−π̂(X)) · (Y − μ̂0(X)) ]
The S-learner plug-in is below the truth. When you add the correction PₙD(P̂), which way does the estimate move?

Double robustness, live: set λ = 0 (outcome model nearly right) and κ = 0 (propensity useless); the correction is tiny and the one-step is still fine. Set λ = 1 and κ = 1: the outcome model is badly wrong and the weights fix it. Set both wrong and nothing saves you.
Act 3

Repeat it 300 times

Same generator, same sliders as Act 2, fresh sample each time. The plug-in's distribution sits where its bias puts it; the one-step's sits on the truth with about the same spread. That is what "removing first-order bias without paying in variance" looks like. Below the histogram, the empirical SE of the one-step is compared to the efficiency bound √(Var D / n), so you can see it is not just unbiased but efficient.

Try n = 100 vs n = 2000 with λ = 0.5, κ = 1. The plug-in's bias does not move with n. The one-step's spread narrows like 1/√n around the truth.

What to say when Alec asks

"The plug-in sits on a curve; its first-order bias is minus the mean of the influence function at the estimate, taken under the truth. I can't compute that mean, but I can estimate it with the sample mean, so I add it back: that is the one-step, one Newton iterate. What's left is the empirical-process term, which is order n−1/2 and gives the efficient asymptotic variance, plus a second-order remainder that is a product of the nuisance errors. For the ATE that remainder is ∫(π̂ − π)(μ̂ − μ)/π̂ dP, so n−1/4 nuisances suffice, and if either one is exactly right the remainder vanishes, which is double robustness. TMLE removes the same first-order term by fluctuating P̂ until the influence-function equation is solved exactly, so it lands on the same expansion as a substitution estimator."
