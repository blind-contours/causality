Scores and Influence, From Scratch

The rest of the course uses four words as if they were obvious: a path through the model, its score h, the pathwise derivative of a parameter, and the influence function. Here each one is built from a single concrete density p(z) on the line. Nothing is abstract: every curve is computed numerically and every number in the readouts is an integral you can check.

The density calculations use one normalized finite grid on [−4,4]. Every score is centered under that same law. The bounded scores and displayed ε range keep pε nonnegative.

Scene 1

A distribution is a point; the model is the set of points you allow

On the left is a density p(z). The whole curve is one point in the model. In a parametric model, say Normal(μ, σ), two numbers pin the curve down, so the Normal model is a two-dimensional sheet with coordinates μ and σ, and a Normal density is a point on it. In a nonparametric model the curve can be any shape: the model is the whole region around the sheet and "a point" is a full function. Move μ and σ and the point slides across the sheet; add the bump and it lifts off.

The bump slider adds a shape that no choice of (μ, σ) can produce. The Normal model has no direction for it; the nonparametric model does. That difference is the whole reason tangent spaces have different sizes. The model picture is schematic: the sheet's grid is exact in μ and σ, the height of the lift is not a distance.
Scene 2

A path: a one-parameter family of densities starting at p

Pick a direction function h(z) and define pε(z) = p(z)·(1 + ε h(z)). At ε = 0 this is p itself; as ε grows the density is tilted, up where h is positive and down where h is negative. This is a submodel: a one-dimensional slice of the model through the point p. The dashed curve is p, the solid curve is pε, and the shading shows the mass being moved.

p_ε(z) = p(z) · (1 + ε·h(z)) (h chosen so that ∫ h p = 0, which keeps ∫ p_ε = 1)
A finite-dimensional parametric model has finitely many independent score directions, though infinitely many paths. The tangent space is the closed linear span of scores of regular allowed paths; in the full nonparametric model it is L²₀(P). A linear path additionally needs a range of ε that preserves nonnegative density.
In one sentenceA submodel is a one-dimensional path through the current distribution. In the nonparametric model the path can go in any direction h with mean zero; in a parametric model only along its coordinates.
Scene 3

The score is the slope of log pε(z) in ε, one number per z

Fix a value z. As ε moves, the density at that z goes up or down. The score at z is the rate: h(z) = d/dε log pε(z) at ε = 0. For the linear tilt that's exactly the h you chose, since log(1 + εh) ≈ εh. So "the score" isn't a mysterious object: it's the collection of these per-z slopes, which is a function of z. Drag the probe to any z and watch the log-density-versus-ε plot on the right; its slope at zero is the value of the score curve below.

For a Normal(μ, σ²) moved along μ, log p_ε(z) = const − (z − μ − ε)²/2σ², whose ε-derivative at 0 is (z − μ)/σ². That's the familiar parametric score, and it is the "tilt" direction here up to scale.
In one sentenceThe score of a path is the derivative of the log-likelihood along it, as a function of the observation. For a parametric model it's the usual score vector; for a nonparametric path it is whatever mean-zero function h the density was tilted by.
Scene 4

What a score averages to, and why

Take any direction h that keeps pε a density (total mass 1 for every ε). Weight h by the density and add it up: what is ∫ h(z) p(z) dz?

∫ pε(z) dz = 1 for every ε. Differentiate in ε: ∫ h(z) p(z) dz = 0. Geometrically, the positive part of h (weighted by p) exactly balances the negative part. That is the fact drawn as "scores are orthogonal to √p" on the sphere. Watch the two shaded areas being measured and cancelled.

d/dε ∫ p_ε = ∫ d/dε [p(1+εh)] = ∫ p·h = E_P[h] = 0
Scene 5

What the parameter does along the path, computed by hand

Take the simplest functional, the mean: ψ(P) = ∫ z p(z) dz. Along the path, ψ(Pε) = ∫ z p(z)(1 + εh(z)) dz = ψ(P) + ε ∫ z h(z) p(z) dz. Because ∫ h p = 0 you can subtract any constant from z inside that integral, so dψ/dε = ∫ (z − ψ) h(z) p(z) dz = EP[ϕ h] with ϕ(z) = z − ψ(P). That ϕ is the influence function of the mean, and it appeared with no estimator in sight: it is a property of the parameter. The scene draws the product ϕ·h·p and integrates it while the mean marker moves.

dψ/dε |₀ = ∫ ϕ(z) h(z) p(z) dz = E_P[ϕ h], ϕ(z) = z − E_P[Z]
Compare the two numbers: the finite difference (ψ(P_ε) − ψ(P))/ε and the inner product E[ϕ h]. For this linear path they agree exactly, because the mean is linear in p. For nonlinear parameters they agree to first order.
In one sentenceThe pathwise derivative of a parameter along a submodel with score h is E[ϕ h], where ϕ is the gradient of the parameter. For the mean, ϕ(z) = z − μ. It comes from differentiating ψ(P_ε) under the integral, not from any estimator.
Scene 6

The same ϕ works for every direction

This is the point of calling ϕ a gradient. Change h and the derivative changes, but you never have to recompute ϕ: the derivative is always ⟨ϕ, h⟩. The scene overlays several directions, shows the derivative in each one as an inner product with the one fixed ϕ, and checks it against the finite difference for each. If a parameter has such a ϕ for all directions in the tangent space, it is pathwise differentiable, and that is the entrance requirement for the whole theory.

In the nonparametric model the tangent space is all square-integrable mean-zero functions, so the gradient is unique: that's why the efficient influence function of the mean is z − μ with no alternatives. In a smaller model there are several gradients, all agreeing on the tangent space; the efficient one is the projection.
Scene 7

Why it's called "influence": one observation's pull on the parameter

Start with something you can hold. The mean of the course's 100 patients is the point where their outcomes balance on a beam. Drop one more patient on it and watch how hard they tip it: that push, per unit of mass, is the influence function.

Take a special path: contaminate P with a tiny point mass at z₀, Pε = (1 − ε)P + ε δz₀. For a discrete mass function with p(z₀)>0, its score is h(z) = 𝟙{z = z₀}/p(z₀) − 1 (a spike minus one). Plugging that score into E[ϕ h] gives exactly ϕ(z₀). (For a continuous density the same contamination is only a formal device, not a regular L² path; see the note below.) So ψ(Pε) ≈ ψ(P) + ε ϕ(z₀): the influence function at z₀ is how much the parameter moves when you add a little mass at z₀. Move the spike and watch the mean move by ε(z₀ − μ). For the mean, the empirical error equals Pn(Z−μ) exactly. For a general asymptotically linear estimator, the leading error is Pnϕ under additional conditions; Pn−P is a signed empirical perturbation, not a single small point-mass contamination.

Draw the influence function from the action

Do not read the formula first. Add a droplet of mass at z₀ and watch the balance point of the density move. Sweep z₀ across the line and record the shift per unit of added mass at each place. The recorded points are the influence function; the formula ϕ(z) = z − ψ names what you already traced.

In one sentenceThe influence function evaluated at z is the Gateaux derivative of the parameter toward a point mass at z: how much one extra observation there would pull the estimate. Averaging it over the sample gives the first-order error of any regular asymptotically linear estimator of this parameter, because in the nonparametric model the influence function is unique.
Scene 8

The same construction for the ATE, term by term

A treated patient's outcome is 1 unit above μ₁(x₀). At x₀ where P(A = 1 | x₀) = 0.2, compared with a place where it is 0.8, how hard does this one observation pull the ATE through its own regression?

Now the observation is Z = (X, A, Y) and the parameter is ψ = ∫ [μ1(x) − μ0(x)] p(x) dx. Contaminate with a point mass at one observation (x₀, a₀, y₀) and ask how ψ moves. Two things change: the distribution of X gets a little more mass at x₀, which moves ψ by μ1(x₀) − μ0(x₀) − ψ; and the regression μa₀ at x₀ gets pulled toward y₀, by the residual scaled by how rare that arm is at x₀, (y₀ − μa₀(x₀)) / P(A = a₀ | x₀), with a sign depending on the arm. Add them and you have the efficient influence function of the ATE, derived by asking "what does one observation do" rather than by looking it up.

ϕ(x₀,a₀,y₀) = [μ₁(x₀) − μ₀(x₀) − ψ] + (2a₀ − 1) · (y₀ − μ_{a₀}(x₀)) / P(A = a₀ | x₀)
Move x₀ to where the chosen arm is rare (a control at high x, say). The residual term grows because 1/P(A = a₀ | x₀) grows: that one observation stands in for many. That is where the inverse-propensity weight comes from, and why positivity matters.
In one sentenceThe EIF of the ATE has two parts because one observation influences the parameter two ways: through the distribution of X, giving μ₁ − μ₀ − ψ, and through the outcome regression at its own x, giving the residual divided by the probability of its arm. The second part is the correction term in AIPW.
Scene 9

For continuous observations, point-mass contamination is a formal derivative device, not generally a regular dominated path with an L² score. The displayed point-mass score is literal for discrete mass functions. The finite-probability laboratory supplies a fully regular discrete construction.

The hardest direction, and why its variance is the bound

Along any one path the problem is parametric in ε, so Cramér–Rao applies: no regular estimator of ψ can have asymptotic variance below (dψ/dε)² / I, with Fisher information I = E[h²]. Substitute dψ/dε = E[ϕ h]: the bound along direction h is E[ϕh]² / E[h²]. By Cauchy–Schwarz that is at most E[ϕ²], with equality when h ∝ ϕ. So the path with score ϕ is the hardest one-dimensional problem hiding inside the nonparametric one, its bound is Var(ϕ), and no regular estimator can beat it because it can't even beat it on that one path. Rotate h between ϕ and something orthogonal and watch the bound.

bound along h = (E[ϕh])² / E[h²] ≤ E[ϕ²] = Var(ϕ), equality iff h ∝ ϕ (the least-favorable submodel)
This is what "least favorable" means, and why TMLE fluctuates along a submodel whose score is the efficient influence function: it is the direction in which the data are least informative about ψ per unit of likelihood, so solving the score equation there is what a maximum-likelihood step would do if it could see ψ.
In one sentenceOn any one-dimensional submodel Cramér–Rao gives a bound of (E[ϕh])²/E[h²]. Maximizing over directions gives Var(ϕ), attained when the score is ϕ itself. That's the efficiency bound and the least-favorable submodel in one sentence.
