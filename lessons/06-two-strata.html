The clever covariate, with 100 people and no calculus you can't see

Two Strata, One Step

One hundred patients. Fifty are young and 45 of them were treated; fifty are old and only 5 were treated. You want the mean outcome if everyone were treated, and your initial outcome model is too low by 1 in both strata. Every scene below asks the same question: if you may move the fitted curve, where should you spend the movement? The answer is "in proportion to 1/π," and that is the clever covariate.

Scene 1

Two strata, equal share of the parameter, unequal evidence

The parameter ψ = E[μ₁(X)] averages the treated-outcome curve over everyone, so each stratum owns exactly half of ψ. But the curve μ₁ is only learned from treated people, and treated people are 45 in the young stratum and 5 in the old. Half the parameter rests on 5 observations.

ψ = 0.5·μ₁(young) + 0.5·μ₁(old) π(young) = 45/50 = 0.9 π(old) = 5/50 = 0.1
Filled dots are treated. The bars on the right are the whole story of this page in one picture: the two strata weigh the same in ψ, but the data that pins down μ₁ is nine times thinner on the right.
Scene 2

Payoff is linear in the shift, cost is quadratic in the shift times the count

Raise the young curve by δY and the old curve by δO. ψ rises by 0.5δY + 0.5δO: that is the payoff, and it does not care how many treated people there are. The likelihood, though, is fitted to treated people only, and moving a curve by δ moves it δ away from each treated person it was fit to. Measured the way the likelihood measures distance (squared, summed over observations), the cost is 45δY² + 5δO². Drag the sliders and watch: the same δ costs nine times more on the left.

payoff(δ_Y, δ_O) = 0.5·δ_Y + 0.5·δ_O cost(δ_Y, δ_O) = 45·δ_Y² + 5·δ_O²
Cost is drawn as one small square per treated person, side δ. Forty-five squares of side 0.33 have the same total area as five squares of side 1.0. The likelihood cannot tell those two moves apart; ψ can.
In one sentencePer unit of likelihood distance, a shift of the outcome curve moves E[μ₁(X)] most where treated people are rare, because the payoff is weighted by the population and the cost by the treated.
Scene 3

Fix the budget, and the best split puts the old curve 9× as far

Hold the cost at a fixed budget B and slide the share of that budget that goes to the old stratum from 0 to 1. The payoff is a curve in that share, and it peaks at a share of 0.9, where δO/δY = 9 = π(young)/π(old). That ratio is the whole point: the curve should move in proportion to 1/π. Change the budget and the peak does not move.

share s to old: 45δ_Y² = (1−s)B, 5δ_O² = sB ⇒ payoff(s) = 0.5√((1−s)B/45) + 0.5√(sB/5), maximum at s = 0.9, δ_O/δ_Y = 9
You have a fixed likelihood budget. To raise ψ the most, how should you split it between the young and old curves?
With B = 50: all-young gives 0.53, all-old gives 1.58, the 0.9 split gives 1.67. All-old is already most of the gain, because 5 people barely constrain that curve at all.
Scene 4

Why it tops out: the price of a strip keeps changing

Cost is one square per treated person, side δ. Growing a square from δ to δ + Δ adds an L-shaped strip whose area is about 2δ·Δ, so the price of the next bit of growth is the square's current side. Shrinking a square frees a strip of the same shape. Start at δY = 0.8, δO = 1.0 (cost 33.8) and run the trade: peel a strip off the 45 young squares, wrap that area around the 5 old squares, keep the total area fixed. Each trade the young squares get smaller, so the strip you peel is shorter and frees less; the old squares get bigger, so the strip you need is longer and eats more. The two strip lengths converge, and when they meet the trade buys nothing. That is the top, at δO = 9δY.

strip freed by shrinking young: 45 · 2δ_Y · Δ = 90δ_Y · Δ strip needed to grow old: 5 · 2δ_O · Δ = 10δ_O · Δ
payoff per unit of shift is flat (0.5 both sides); only the strip prices move ⇒ stop when 90δ_Y = 10δ_O
The scrub runs a little past the optimum on purpose. Past it, the strip you peel off young is shorter than the strip old needs, so the same area buys less shift than you gave up and the payoff curve turns down.
In one sentenceMarginal cost in the likelihood metric is proportional to how far you have already moved, times how many observations you moved away from. Payoff to ψ is flat. Reallocating movement toward sparse strata pays until the marginal costs equalize, which is δ ∝ 1/π.
Scene 5

Why 9: equalize the marginal cost of the next unit of payoff

At the optimum, the last bit of budget must buy the same payoff wherever it is spent. The cost of the young curve is 45δY², whose slope is 2·45·δY = 90δY: that is how much cost the next tiny bit of young shift adds. The old slope is 10δO. Both strata add 0.5 of payoff per unit of shift, so "same payoff per unit cost" means 90δY = 10δO, i.e. δO = 9δY. Now the good part: if you write the shift as δ = ε/π (young: ε/0.9, old: ε/0.1), the two slopes are 100ε and 100ε at every ε. Moving along 1/π keeps the marginal costs equal the whole way. That ray is the least-favorable path, and TMLE only has to choose how far along it to go.

d/dδ_Y (45δ_Y²) = 90δ_Y d/dδ_O (5δ_O²) = 10δ_O with δ_Y = ε/0.9, δ_O = ε/0.1: 90·(ε/0.9) = 100ε = 10·(ε/0.1)
The tangent lines are the marginal costs. On the 1/π path they stay parallel; on the equal-shift path the young tangent is nine times steeper, which says: you are paying nine times more for the same payoff on the left, so move the budget right.
In one sentenceThe efficient influence function's outcome term is H·(Y − μ), H = A/π. Fluctuating μ along H is moving along the ray where marginal likelihood cost per unit of ψ is equal across covariate values; every other direction wastes likelihood on a part of the curve that ψ barely uses.
Scene 6

Now let the data choose ε: the score equation and the 50/50 evidence

Real residuals, ri = Yi − μ̂(Xi), scattered around 1. Fluctuate με = μ̂ + ε·H with H = 1/π and pick ε by least squares. The score equation Σ Hi(ri − εHi) = 0 gives ε̂ = ΣHiri / ΣHi². Look at the numerator: the young stratum contributes 45 × 1.11 × r̄Y ≈ 50·r̄Y and the old contributes 5 × 10 × r̄O = 50·r̄O. The weighting makes the 5 old residuals count exactly as much as the 45 young ones, because each stratum is half of ψ. Then ψ moves by ε̂·(0.5/0.9 + 0.5/0.1) = ε̂ · 5.56, which is precisely the one-step correction Pₙ[H r].

ε̂ = Σ Hᵢ rᵢ / Σ Hᵢ² ψ_TMLE − ψ_plug = ε̂ · Pₙ[H(1,X)] = ε̂·(0.5/0.9 + 0.5/0.1) one-step: ψ_plug + Pₙ[H r]
With noise SD 0 both residual means are 1, the two evidence bars are exactly 50 and 50, ε̂ = 0.18, the young curve moves 0.2 and the old curve 1.8, and ψ moves by exactly 1.0, which is the bias you were told the initial fit had. Turn the noise up and see the same machine run on messy data.
In one sentenceThe clever covariate reweights each residual by 1/π so that every part of the covariate space contributes evidence in proportion to its share of the target, not its share of the treated. Solving the score equation along that fluctuation is the TMLE update, and for this Gaussian fluctuation it lands exactly on the one-step estimate.
Scene 7

Drag the treated count to zero: positivity, and why the estimand picks the direction

Everything above assumed 5 treated old patients. Slide that number down. The cost of moving the old curve is k·δO², so as k falls the curve gets cheaper to move, the clever covariate 50/k grows, and at k = 0 the curve is free: no observation resists any move at all, so the data say nothing about μ1(old), and ψ = 0.5μ1(young) + 0.5μ1(old) is not identified. That is what a positivity violation looks like from inside the estimator. Then switch the estimand. If the target weights strata by their treated share instead of their population share (the mean outcome among the treated, the E[Y(1) | A = 1] half of the ATT), the payoff weights become 45/(45+k) and k/(45+k), the population share cancels against the treated count, and the optimal direction is a constant: every curve moves the same amount. The estimand decides the direction; the data only decide how far.

ATE-type target: payoff 0.5δ_Y + 0.5δ_O, cost 45δ_Y² + kδ_O² ⇒ δ_O/δ_Y = 45/k = π_Y/π_O
Treated-weighted target: payoff (45/(45+k))δ_Y + (k/(45+k))δ_O ⇒ δ_O/δ_Y = 1
As the number of treated old patients k goes to 0, what happens to the optimal ratio δ_O/δ_Y for the ATE?
Positivity is usually stated as π(x) > 0 for all x. Here you can see why: it is the condition that every part of the curve ψ depends on is resisted by at least some data.
In one sentencePositivity is what makes the least-favorable direction finite. Where π is zero the clever covariate is infinite, the fit can move without likelihood cost, and the parameter is not identified. Changing the estimand changes the payoff weights, and therefore the direction: the ATT does not need positivity where there are no treated people, because those people carry no weight.
