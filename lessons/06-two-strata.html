The clever covariate, with 100 people and no calculus you can't see

Two Strata, One Step

One hundred patients. Fifty are young and 45 of them were treated; fifty are old and only 5 were treated. You want the mean outcome if everyone were treated, and your initial outcome model is too low by 1 in both strata. Every scene below asks the same question: if you may move the fitted curve, where should you spend the movement? The answer is "in proportion to 1/π," and that is the clever covariate.

Scene 1

Two strata, equal share of the parameter, unequal evidence

The parameter ψ = E[μ₁(X)] averages the treated-outcome curve over everyone, so each stratum owns exactly half of ψ. But the curve μ₁ is only learned from treated people, and treated people are 45 in the young stratum and 5 in the old. Half the parameter rests on 5 observations.

ψ = 0.5·μ₁(young) + 0.5·μ₁(old) π(young) = 45/50 = 0.9 π(old) = 5/50 = 0.1
Filled dots are treated. The bars on the right are the whole story of this page in one picture: the two strata weigh the same in ψ, but the data that pins down μ₁ is nine times thinner on the right.
Scene 2

Payoff is linear in the shift, cost is quadratic in the shift times the count

Raise the young curve by δY and the old curve by δO. The target rises by 0.5δY+0.5δO. For independent unit-variance Gaussian observations, twice the KL distance from the initial model is 45δY²+5δO². This is the information metric used to compare directions, not the observed loss change from a biased fit. The actual change in squared-error loss is Σₛ[nₛδₛ²−2δₛΣᵢrᵢ]. Residuals determine the step size.

payoff(δ_Y, δ_O) = 0.5·δ_Y + 0.5·δ_O information cost(δ_Y, δ_O) = 45·δ_Y² + 5·δ_O²
Cost is drawn as one small square per treated person, side δ. Forty-five squares of side ⅓ have the same total area as five squares of side 1 (both 5). At the starting setting, the young curve moves ⅓ and costs 5, while the old curve moves 3 and its five squares cost 45. The likelihood cannot tell those two moves apart; ψ can.
In one sentencePer unit of likelihood distance, a shift of the outcome curve moves E[μ₁(X)] most where treated people are rare, because the payoff is weighted by the population and the cost by the treated.
Scene 3

The budget plane: the best move lies on the ray δO = 9δY

Put δY on the horizontal axis and δO on the vertical. Every move you can make is a point in this plane. The moves that cost the same budget B form an ellipse, 45δY² + 5δO² = B, squashed hard in the δY direction because 45 people resist that shift and only 5 resist the other. The payoff 0.5δY + 0.5δO = c is a straight line of slope −1. Spending the budget well means finding the highest payoff line that still touches the ellipse: the point where it is tangent.

You have a fixed likelihood budget. To raise ψ the most, how should you split it between the young and old curves?
tangent where ∇cost ∥ ∇payoff: (90δ_Y, 10δ_O) ∥ (0.5, 0.5) ⇒ 90δ_Y = 10δ_O ⇒ δ_O / δ_Y = 9 = π_Y / π_O
Play grows the budget. The ellipse swells, but the purple tangent point slides out along the same ray every time: the split is a direction, and the budget only decides how far along it to go.

Why the ray is 1/π, in one line: write the shift as δ = ε/π (young ε/0.9, old ε/0.1). The marginal costs are then 90·(ε/0.9) = 100ε and 10·(ε/0.1) = 100ε, equal at every ε. Along that ray the last bit of budget buys the same payoff in both strata, so there is nothing left to reallocate. That ray is the least-favorable path, and TMLE only has to choose ε.

In one sentenceThe least-favourable direction is the ray along which each cost ellipse is tangent to a payoff line, and that ray is δ ∝ 1/π: the clever covariate is a direction in this plane, and the budget only decides how far along it to go.
See it another way: fix the budget and slide the share

Hold the cost at B and slide the share s of that budget that goes to the old stratum from 0 to 1. The payoff peaks at s = 0.9, where δO/δY = 9. Change the budget and the peak does not move.

45δ_Y² = (1−s)B, 5δ_O² = sB ⇒ payoff(s) = 0.5√((1−s)B/45) + 0.5√(sB/5), maximum at s = 0.9
With B = 50: all-young gives 0.53, all-old gives 1.58, the 0.9 split gives 1.67.
See it another way: trade strips between the squares

Cost is one square per treated person, side δ. Growing a square from δ to δ + Δ adds an L-shaped strip of area about 2δ·Δ, so the price of the next bit of shift is the square's current side. Peel strips off the 45 young squares and wrap the same area around the 5 old squares. The strip you free keeps shrinking and the strip you need keeps growing; when they are equal (90δY = 10δO) the trade buys nothing more.

The scrub runs a little past the optimum on purpose, so you can see the payoff turn down.
See it another way: equal marginal costs along δ = ε/π

The cost curves 45δ² and 5δ² are parabolas; their slopes 90δY and 10δO are the marginal costs. Step along δ = ε/π and the two tangent lines stay parallel. Step with equal shifts and the young tangent is nine times steeper.

In one sentenceThe efficient influence function's outcome term is H·(Y − μ), H = A/π. Fluctuating μ along H is moving along the ray where marginal likelihood cost per unit of ψ is equal across covariate values; every other direction wastes likelihood on a part of the curve that ψ barely uses.
Scene 4

Now let the data choose ε: the score equation and the 50/50 evidence

Real residuals, ri = Yi − μ̂(Xi), scattered around 1. Fluctuate με = μ̂ + ε·H with H = 1/π and pick ε by least squares. The score equation Σ Hi(ri − εHi) = 0 gives ε̂ = ΣHiri / ΣHi². Look at the numerator: the young stratum contributes 45 × 1.11 × r̄Y ≈ 50·r̄Y and the old contributes 5 × 10 × r̄O = 50·r̄O. The weighting makes the 5 old residuals count exactly as much as the 45 young ones, because each stratum is half of ψ. Then ψ moves by ε̂·(0.5/0.9 + 0.5/0.1) = ε̂ · 5.56, which is precisely the one-step correction Pₙ[H r].

ε̂ = Σ Hᵢ rᵢ / Σ Hᵢ² ψ_TMLE − ψ_plug = ε̂ · Pₙ[H(1,X)] = ε̂·(0.5/0.9 + 0.5/0.1) one-step: ψ_plug + Pₙ[H r]
With noise SD 0 both residual means are 1, the two evidence bars are exactly 50 and 50, ε̂ = 0.18, the young curve moves 0.2 and the old curve 1.8, and ψ moves by exactly 1.0, which is the bias you were told the initial fit had. Turn the noise up and see the same machine run on messy data.
In one sentenceThe clever covariate reweights each residual by 1/π so that every part of the covariate space contributes evidence in proportion to its share of the target, not its share of the treated. Solving the score equation along that fluctuation is the TMLE update, and in this empirically calibrated two-stratum example it equals the one-step estimate. General Gaussian fluctuations need not do so.
Scene 5

When the old treated count runs out

Everything above leaned on the 5 treated old patients. Each of them stands in for 1/π̂ = 50/5 = 10 old patients, because ψ needs μ₁ for all 50 of them. Now let that count shrink. With k treated old patients the weight of each is 50/k, so fewer patients carry more of ψ each, and the one-step correction and the fluctuation both lean harder on them. At k = 0 there is nobody left to carry it.

Suppose only one treated old patient is left. What happens to the weight 1/π̂ that patient carries?

Two different things can go wrong, and they need different words. Population positivity fails when g(old) = 0: no older person could ever be treated, so μ₁(old) is not identified by any amount of data, and E[Y(1)] is not identified either because the old stratum has positive weight in it. An empty sample cell (k = 0 with g(old) > 0) is a finite-sample problem: μ₁(old) is identified in the population, but this sample carries no treated evidence about it, so any estimate there comes from the model's extrapolation, not from data. Near-violations (k small, weights large) sit between the two: the estimate exists but its variance explodes. An ATT contrast would instead need controls wherever treated people occur; changing the target changes which support is required. The positivity lesson takes this further: diagnosing weights, truncation, and changing the estimand.

What is exact here: the arithmetic of Scenes 2 to 4 (payoffs, costs, ε̂ and the one-step correction). What is simulated: the residuals in Scene 4 (press New sample). What is schematic: the unit-variance Gaussian information metric, and the fixed stratum sizes of 50.
