Two variables, two operations, one table

Differentiating Under the Integral

"The derivative passes inside the integral" is a statement about a table with two axes. Down the side is ε, the position along the path; across the top is z, the position on the line. Each cell holds one number, z·p(z)(1 + εh(z))·dz. Integrating over z means adding up a row. Differentiating in ε means comparing one row with the next. The whole point is that you can do those two things in either order.

f(z, ε) = z · p(z) · (1 + ε h(z)) · dz ← one cell
ψ(ε) = Σ_z f(z, ε) ← add up a row
dψ/dε = Σ_z ∂f/∂ε (z, ε) = Σ_z z · p(z) · h(z) · dz ← add up the row of per-cell slopes

For a finite sum, exchanging derivative and sum is exact. For an integral, regularity such as an integrable dominating function is needed. The displayed midpoint bins approximate a normalized reference density; tail omission and quadrature error are not a failure of differentiation.

Scene 1

The table: z across, ε down, one number per cell

Every cell is the contribution of one z-bin to the mean, at one value of ε. Color shows sign and size. A row is one distribution on the path; its sum is ψ(Pε), printed at the right. Move the highlighted row with the slider and read the row sums: that column of numbers is the function ψ(ε) you're about to differentiate.

z and ε are independent axes. Changing ε moves you to a different row; it does not change which z the columns stand for. That independence is all Leibniz's rule needs.
Scene 2

Two ways to get the slope of ψ: subtract row sums, or sum row differences

Take the row at ε and the row at ε + Δ. Way A: add up each row, then subtract the two totals and divide by Δ. Way B: subtract the rows cell by cell, divide each difference by Δ, then add up. They give the same number, because a sum of differences is the difference of the sums. "Passing the derivative inside the integral" is choosing Way B. Watch both computed side by side.

Way A: [ Σ_z f(z, ε+Δ) − Σ_z f(z, ε) ] / Δ
Way B: Σ_z [ f(z, ε+Δ) − f(z, ε) ] / Δ ← same number, by linearity of Σ
Scene 3

Inside one cell: f is a straight line in ε, with slope z·p(z)·h(z)

Look down a single column of the table, one fixed z. The cell values as ε varies are z·p(z)·(1 + εh(z))·dz: a constant plus ε times a constant. That's a straight line in ε, and its slope is z·p(z)·h(z)·dz. There is no limit to take and nothing to approximate; the "derivative inside" is just reading off the coefficient of ε. Pick a column and see its line.

For other functionals the cell is not exactly linear in ε and the per-cell derivative is a genuine limit; the argument is the same, with a remainder that is second order in ε.
Scene 4

Add up the per-cell slopes: that sum is ∫ z h p dz, and it is EP[Z·h(Z)]

Take the slope of every column and add them. The result is Σz z·h(z)·p(z)·dz, which in the limit of thin bins is ∫ z h(z) p(z) dz. The p(z)dz in each term is the probability of that bin, so this is a probability-weighted average of the function z·h(z): an expectation. Then the free constant: subtracting ψ from z changes nothing because Σ ψ·h·p·dz = ψ·0. What remains is EP[(Z − ψ)h(Z)] = EP[ϕh]. The scene shows the three rows again, this time being read as "value × probability, summed."

Shrink dz: more bins, the same sum. That's the sense in which Σ becomes ∫. The expectation was there at every bin width; it just gets more exact.
In one sentenceψ(P_ε) is a row sum over z; its derivative in ε is the same row sum applied to the per-cell derivatives, because sums and derivatives are both linear and act on different variables. Each cell's derivative is z·h·p·dz, so the slope is Σ z h p dz = E[Zh]. Centering with the mean-zero score gives E[(Z − ψ)h].
