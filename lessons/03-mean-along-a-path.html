One derivation, six moves, each one drawn

The Mean Along a Path

The claim to be understood: for the mean ψ(P) = ∫ z p(z) dz, the rate of change along any path with score h is EP[ϕ h] with ϕ(z) = z − ψ(P). Every step of the algebra is a picture, and every picture is computed from the same density p(z). Each scene animates exactly one line of the derivation.

ψ(P_ε) = ∫ z · p(z)(1 + ε h(z)) dz
       = ∫ z p(z) dz + ε ∫ z h(z) p(z) dz (move 3: split)
       = ψ(P) + ε ∫ (z − c) h(z) p(z) dz for any c (move 4: ∫ h p = 0)
       = ψ(P) + ε ∫ ϕ(z) h(z) p(z) dz, ϕ(z) = z − ψ(P) (move 5: choose c = ψ)
⇒ dψ(P_ε)/dε |₀ = E_P[ϕ h]

Fine-grid identities use a normalized probability grid on [−4,4]. Coarse midpoint bars on [−3.5,3.5] approximate that distribution and omit tail mass: their arithmetic is an approximation, not an exact discretization of the same law. Use the three-outcome laboratory for exact finite sums.

Move 1

ψ(P) = ∫ z p(z) dz is a weighted sum: position times mass, added up

Cut the line into small bins of width dz. Each bin holds mass p(z)dz. The mean is the sum over bins of (position of the bin) × (mass in the bin). Watch the bins being added one at a time; the running total lands on the balance point of the density.

Negative positions contribute negative terms. That's why the running total can go down before it goes up.
Move 2

Along the path the masses change; the positions do not

On the path pε(z) = p(z)(1 + εh(z)), each bin's mass becomes p(z)(1 + εh(z))dz. The bin positions z are the same as before. So the mean along the path is the same weighted sum with new weights. Move ε and watch the bar heights change and the balance point slide.

Move 3

Split each bin's contribution into "the old part" and "ε times the new part"

z·p(z)(1 + εh(z)) = z·p(z) + ε·z·h(z)·p(z). Bin by bin, the integrand is the old integrand plus ε times a second integrand that does not depend on ε. Summing gives ψ(P) + ε·(a fixed number). That is why ψ(Pε) is exactly a straight line in ε for this path: the slope is that fixed number, ∫ z h p dz.

z·p·(1+εh) = z·p + ε·z·h·p ⇒ ψ(P_ε) = ψ(P) + ε·∫ z h p dz
Split each bin's contribution into an old part and an ε part. What happens to ψ as ε grows?
Move 4

You may subtract any constant from z inside the second integral

∫ (z − c)·h·p dz = ∫ z·h·p dz − c·∫ h·p dz, and the last integral is zero because h is a score. So the slope doesn't care which c you subtract. Drag c: the shaded product changes shape everywhere, but its signed area is fixed. This is the step that turns "z" into "z minus something."

∫ (z − c) h p dz = ∫ z h p dz − c·∫ h p dz = ∫ z h p dz − c·0
Try c far from the data: the product grows huge and lopsided, and the area still doesn't move. The cancellation is exact because ∫ h p = 0 is exact.
Move 5

Choose c = ψ(P). The thing multiplying h is now ϕ(z) = z − ψ(P)

Any c works, so pick the one that makes the multiplier mean zero: c = ψ(P). Then the slope is ∫ ϕ h p dz = EP[ϕ h] with ϕ(z) = z − ψ(P). On a grid this is a dot product: the vector of ϕ values, times the vector of h values, times the weights p, summed. The scene shows the three rows of bars, their elementwise product, and the sum, next to the slope of ψ(Pε) that it equals.

dψ/dε = Σᵢ ϕ(zᵢ) · h(zᵢ) · p(zᵢ) dz = ⟨ϕ, h⟩_P
Only 24 bins are drawn so you can see each product; the readout uses the fine grid. Same number either way, up to discretization.
Move 6

Why this ϕ and not another: it must work for every h, and have mean zero

Could a different function ϕ̃ give the same slope? Suppose ϕ̃ = ϕ + g. Then ∫ ϕ̃ h p − ∫ ϕ h p = ∫ g h p. For this to vanish for every mean-zero h, g has to be constant (a constant is orthogonal to all mean-zero h, and nothing else is). And the convention that influence functions have mean zero pins the constant to 0. So in the nonparametric model ϕ(z) = z − ψ(P) is the only influence function of the mean: the efficient one, with variance Var(Z). Pick a g and watch ∫ g h p fail to vanish for some h, unless g is constant.

In a smaller model (fewer allowed h) the test is weaker, so more g pass it and there are several influence functions; the efficient one is the projection onto the allowed directions. In the nonparametric model the test is as strong as it gets and ϕ is unique.
In one sentenceDifferentiating the mean along a path with score h gives ∫ z h p, and since scores have mean zero, z can be centered at the mean for free: dψ/dε = E[(Z − μ) h]. That identifies ϕ(z) = z − μ as the gradient; it's unique in the nonparametric model because it has to reproduce the derivative for every mean-zero h.
