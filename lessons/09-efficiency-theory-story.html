Semiparametric efficiency theory in nine moving pictures

Efficiency Theory, Drawn

The story of how you get from "a parameter of a distribution" to "an estimator with a known sampling distribution and the smallest possible variance," told in the geometry Schuler and van der Laan use in Introduction to Modern Causal Inference. Each scene has a Play button where the clock moves a mathematical state, a Reveal button where it only uncovers annotations, a scrubber, and the one or two dials that matter. Under each scene is the sentence to say out loud.

Scene 1

A parameter is a map from distributions to numbers

The model 𝓜 is the set of distributions allowed by your assumptions. The estimand ψ takes each one to a number: ψ(P) = EP[μ1(X) − μ0(X)], say. Drawn on 𝓜, that's a landscape with level curves. Everything later in the story is about how that landscape behaves near the truth.

This is a finite-dimensional illustration. The polynomial landscape is made into a two-parameter Gaussian location model in scene 8; the visible blob is a viewing window. General nonparametric models may be infinite dimensional; parametric models may have any finite dimension.
In one sentenceThe estimand is a functional, a smooth map from the model to the reals. Identification is what allows the claim that its value at the truth is the causal quantity; estimation is about how the map bends near the truth.
Scene 2

An estimator is asymptotically linear if its error is an average

An estimator ψ̂(Pn) is asymptotically linear when ψ̂ − ψ(P) = Pnϕ + oP(n−1/2) for some mean-zero function ϕ of one observation: the influence function. With independent observations and finite influence-function variance, the central limit theorem supplies the limiting distribution. Watch a real sample: each point contributes ϕ(Zi), the error is their average, and across repetitions the average is asymptotically normal with variance Var(ϕ)/n.

The influence function is literally each observation's contribution to the estimator's error. Its variance is the estimator's asymptotic variance; its empirical sd over √n is your standard error.
In one sentenceAsymptotic linearity means the estimator behaves like a sample mean of its influence function. That's what gives a normal limit, and Var(ϕ) is the number we'll try to make as small as possible.
Scene 3

The pathwise derivative, and the gradient that generates it

Move away from P along a path Pε with score h. The parameter changes at some rate, the pathwise derivative. For a smooth parameter that rate is an inner product, d/dε ψ(Pε) = EP[ϕ h], the same ϕ for every direction. That ϕ is the gradient. Rotate the direction and watch the derivative rise and fall as the cosine of the angle to the gradient.

Regularity of an estimator is the requirement that its limit not depend on which of these paths the truth drifts along. The Hájek–Le Cam convolution theorem then says the best regular estimator has the gradient as its influence function.
In one sentenceA pathwise-differentiable parameter has a gradient: one function whose covariance with any score gives the derivative of ψ in that direction. The efficient influence function is that gradient, taken in the tangent space.
Scene 4

Scores are tangent to the model

Put each density at the point √p so that every distribution lies on the unit sphere of L₂. A path through the model is a curve on the sphere; its velocity at p is ½h√p, tangent to the sphere and so orthogonal to √p. That orthogonality is exactly EP[h] = 0. The closed linear span of the path scores is the tangent space 𝒯 at P; it changes as P moves, and it gets bigger as the model gets bigger.

In an RCT the treatment mechanism is known, so paths can't move it: the tangent space is missing those directions. Observational data adds them. At the same observed law, enlarging a model can increase the bound or leave it unchanged. For the population ATE, the EIF is already orthogonal to treatment-mechanism scores: knowing the propensity alone leaves its bound unchanged. Changing the actual assignment probabilities changes the law and can change the bound.
In one sentenceScores are the velocities of paths through P; they live in the tangent space, whose finite-dimensional illustration is a plane touching the sphere at √p. Knowing the propensity removes directions. Whether the bound changes depends on the target; for the population ATE at the same law it does not.
Scene 5

Gradients form an affine set; projection finds its shortest member

Any two influence functions of a regular estimator have the same covariance with every score, so their difference is orthogonal to 𝒯. The set Φ of mean-zero gradients is therefore D* + 𝒯⊥. It is a line only when that orthogonal complement is one dimensional, as drawn here. It meets 𝒯 in exactly one point, ϕ†, and by Pythagoras that point has the smallest norm, which is the smallest variance. That's the efficiency bound.

An inefficient RAL estimator, say IPW with a known propensity, has an influence function somewhere up or down this line. Augmenting it (AIPW) is projecting it onto 𝒯.
In one sentenceEvery influence function is the efficient one plus something orthogonal to the tangent space, and orthogonal pieces only add variance. So the efficient influence function is the projection of any gradient onto the tangent space, and its variance is the bound.
Scene 6

Plug-in bias, and one step to remove it

Fit nuisances, get P̂, evaluate ψ(P̂): the plug-in. Walk the straight path from P̂ to the truth P and lift it: the curve is ψ(P̂ + ε(P − P̂)). Its tangent at P̂ has slope Pϕ̂, which you cannot compute, and sample slope Pnϕ̂, which you can. Add the sample slope back and the first-order bias is gone. Three gaps remain visible: plug-in bias (removed), empirical process (order n−1/2), second-order remainder (order of the squared nuisance error).

Halve the nuisance error: the first-order term halves, the remainder quarters. Efficient centered inference needs R₂=oₚ(n−1/2). Two errors exactly n−1/4 do not by themselves meet that condition.
In one sentenceThe plug-in sits on a curve; its first-order error is minus the mean of the influence function at the estimate. Estimate that mean with the sample and add it back: that is the one-step, and for the ATE it is AIPW. What's left is an empirical-process term at root-n and a remainder that is second order in the nuisance error.
Scene 7

Double robustness is a rectangle

For the ATE, using Ψ(P̂)−Ψ(P₀)=−P₀D*(P̂)+R₂, the remainder is E₀[(ĝ−g₀){(m̂₁−m₁₀)/ĝ+(m̂₀−m₀₀)/(1−ĝ)}]. With inverse propensities bounded, its magnitude is bounded by a constant times the product of nuisance L² errors. The rectangle depicts that magnitude bound, not the signed exact remainder. Exactly n−1/4 errors give a product at the same order as sampling error; efficient inference needs a negligible product or a sharper argument.

The plug-in's error, by contrast, is a single side: the whole of ‖μ̂ − μ‖, at the outcome model's own rate. That's why a slow biased outcome fit alone need not justify √n inference. The correction helps under additional conditions.
In one sentenceThe remainder is a product of nuisance errors. If either is exactly right the product is zero, which is double robustness; if both converge faster than n to the minus one-quarter, the product is smaller than n to the minus one-half, which is what makes the estimator asymptotically linear and efficient.
Scene 8

TMLE walks through the model; the one-step jumps in ψ

Here is a fully specified finite model: Z follows N₂(θ,I), and Ψ(θ)=2+0.9θ₁+0.5θ₂+c(0.6θ₁²−0.7θ₁θ₂+0.2θ₂²). Its EIF is ∇Ψ(θ)·(Z−θ). The information metric on θ is Euclidean. A line θ+ε∇Ψ(θ) is locally least favorable, and Gaussian likelihood gives ε̂=∇Ψ(θ)·(Z̄−θ)/‖∇Ψ(θ)‖². Fit that line once, or recompute the direction and iterate until the current EIF equation is small. The point labelled Pₙ represents its sufficient statistic Z̄ in this model, not an empirical density in the Gaussian family.

A single line fit zeros its own likelihood score. For nonlinear Ψ it need not zero the updated EIF. Iterating local Gaussian line fits targets that updated equation; this is not labelled a universal submodel. Equality with the one-step when c=0 is specific to this Gaussian linear-target example.
In one sentenceTMLE fluctuates the initial fit along a least-favorable submodel until the empirical mean of the efficient influence function is zero, then plugs in. The one-step never moves in 𝓜 at all; it corrects the number. TMLE applies the same first-order correction to the distribution instead, so the estimate stays inside the parameter space.
Scene 9

Inference: test the assumptions behind the promise

Bias, variance and coverage need separate checks. One correct nuisance model can give consistency without efficiency. Try the four correctness cases and compare the empirical spread with the efficient bound; fitted-nuisance intervals can also fail under misspecification.

Geometry follows Schuler & van der Laan, Introduction to Modern Causal Inference, ch. 3–4; the influence-function projection picture is Tsiatis's; the rectangle in scene 7 is the Cauchy–Schwarz bound on the ATE remainder as in Kennedy's notes and Chernozhukov et al. (2018).
