Semiparametric efficiency theory in nine moving pictures

Efficiency Theory, Drawn

The story of how you get from "a parameter of a distribution" to "an estimator with a known sampling distribution and the smallest possible variance," told in the geometry Schuler and van der Laan use in Introduction to Modern Causal Inference. Each scene has a Play button that runs the reveal, a scrubber, and the one or two dials that matter. Under each scene is the sentence to say out loud.

Scene 1

A parameter is a map from distributions to numbers

The model 𝓜 is the set of distributions you're willing to believe. The estimand ψ takes each one to a number: ψ(P) = EP[μ1(X) − μ0(X)], say. Drawn on 𝓜, that's a landscape with level curves. Everything later in the story is about how that landscape behaves near the truth.

Nothing here is an estimate yet. This is the object: a smooth function on an infinite-dimensional set. "Nonparametric" means 𝓜 is the whole blob; a parametric model would be a one-dimensional curve inside it.
In one sentenceThe estimand is a functional, a smooth map from the model to the reals. Identification is what lets me claim its value at the truth is the causal quantity; estimation is about how the map bends near the truth.
Scene 2

An estimator is asymptotically linear if its error is an average

An estimator ψ̂(Pn) is asymptotically linear when ψ̂ − ψ(P) = Pnϕ + oP(n−1/2) for some mean-zero function ϕ of one observation: the influence function. Then the central limit theorem gives you the sampling distribution for free. Watch a real sample: each point contributes ϕ(Zi), the error is their average, and across repetitions the average is normal with variance Var(ϕ)/n.

The influence function is literally each observation's contribution to the estimator's error. Its variance is the estimator's asymptotic variance; its empirical sd over √n is your standard error.
In one sentenceAsymptotic linearity means the estimator behaves like a sample mean of its influence function. That's what gives a normal limit, and Var(ϕ) is the number we'll try to make as small as possible.
Scene 3

The pathwise derivative, and the gradient that generates it

Move away from P along a path Pε with score h. The parameter changes at some rate, the pathwise derivative. For a smooth parameter that rate is an inner product, d/dε ψ(Pε) = EP[ϕ h], the same ϕ for every direction. That ϕ is the gradient. Rotate the direction and watch the derivative rise and fall as the cosine of the angle to the gradient.

Regularity of an estimator is the requirement that its limit not depend on which of these paths the truth drifts along. The Hájek–Le Cam convolution theorem then says the best regular estimator has the gradient as its influence function.
In one sentenceA pathwise-differentiable parameter has a gradient: one function whose covariance with any score gives the derivative of ψ in that direction. The efficient influence function is that gradient, taken in the tangent space.
Scene 4

Scores are tangent to the model

Put each density at the point √p so that every distribution lies on the unit sphere of L₂. A path through the model is a curve on the sphere; its velocity at p is h√p, tangent to the sphere and so orthogonal to √p. That orthogonality is exactly EP[h] = 0. The set of all such velocities is the tangent space 𝒯 at P; it changes as P moves, and it gets bigger as the model gets bigger.

In an RCT the treatment mechanism is known, so paths can't move it: the tangent space is missing those directions. Observational data adds them. A bigger tangent space means the efficient influence function has more to be orthogonal to, and the efficiency bound gets larger.
In one sentenceScores are the velocities of paths through P; they live in the tangent space, which is the plane touching the sphere at √p. Knowing the propensity in a trial removes directions from that plane, which is why trials have a smaller efficiency bound.
Scene 5

All influence functions form a line; the efficient one is its projection onto the tangent space

Any two influence functions of a regular estimator have the same covariance with every score, so their difference is orthogonal to 𝒯. The set Φ of influence functions is therefore a line parallel to 𝒯⊥. It meets 𝒯 in exactly one point, ϕ†, and by Pythagoras that point has the smallest norm, which is the smallest variance. That's the efficiency bound.

An inefficient RAL estimator, say IPW with a known propensity, has an influence function somewhere up or down this line. Augmenting it (AIPW) is projecting it onto 𝒯.
In one sentenceEvery influence function is the efficient one plus something orthogonal to the tangent space, and orthogonal pieces only add variance. So the efficient influence function is the projection of any gradient onto the tangent space, and its variance is the bound.
Scene 6

Plug-in bias, and one step to remove it

Fit nuisances, get P̂, evaluate ψ(P̂): the plug-in. Walk the straight path from P̂ to the truth P and lift it: the curve is ψ(P̂ + ε(P − P̂)). Its tangent at P̂ has slope −Pϕ̂, which you cannot compute, and sample slope −Pnϕ̂, which you can. Add the sample slope back and the first-order bias is gone. Three gaps remain visible: plug-in bias (removed), empirical process (order n−1/2), second-order remainder (order of the squared nuisance error).

Halve the nuisance error: the first-order term halves, the remainder quarters. That asymmetry is why nuisances at n−1/4 deliver a target at n−1/2.
In one sentenceThe plug-in sits on a curve; its first-order error is minus the mean of the influence function at the estimate. I estimate that mean with the sample and add it back: that's the one-step, and for the ATE it's AIPW. What's left is an empirical-process term at root-n and a remainder that is second order in the nuisance error.
Scene 7

Double robustness is a rectangle

For the ATE the remainder is R2 = ∫ (π̂ − π)(μ̂ − μ) / π̂ dP, so up to constants it's bounded by the product of the two nuisance errors: the area of a rectangle whose sides are ‖π̂ − π‖ and ‖μ̂ − μ‖. Set either side to zero and the area is zero regardless of the other: that's double robustness. Let both sides shrink like n−1/4 and the area shrinks like n−1/2: that's the rate condition.

The plug-in's error, by contrast, is a single side: the whole of ‖μ̂ − μ‖, at the outcome model's own rate. That's why a regularized outcome model alone can't give √n inference and a corrected one can.
In one sentenceThe remainder is a product of nuisance errors. If either is exactly right the product is zero, which is double robustness; if both converge faster than n to the minus one-quarter, the product is smaller than n to the minus one-half, which is what makes the estimator asymptotically linear and efficient.
Scene 8

TMLE walks through the model; the one-step jumps in ψ

Same landscape as scene 1. The efficient influence function at a point is the gradient: perpendicular to the level curves, the direction of fastest change in ψ per unit likelihood. That's the least-favorable direction. TMLE starts at P̂, moves along the current gradient by the ε̂ that solves the score equation on that line, recomputes the gradient, and repeats until PnϕP̃ = 0. Then it plugs in. The one-step never moves in 𝓜 at all; it corrects the number. With straight level curves they agree exactly; with curvature they differ by a second-order amount.

The fixed-direction path is the classic one-dimensional submodel through P̂ with score ϕ̂; the universal path re-aims at every point so its score is always the current EIF. Both end where the empirical score equation is solved.
In one sentenceTMLE fluctuates the initial fit along a least-favorable submodel until the empirical mean of the efficient influence function is zero, then plugs in. Same first-order correction as the one-step, applied to the distribution instead of to the number, so the estimate stays inside the parameter space.
Scene 9

Inference: the sampling distribution you were promised

Repeat the whole procedure on many samples. The plug-in's distribution sits at its bias; the one-step's sits on the truth with sd matching √(Var ϕ / n), and the influence-function standard error gives intervals with nominal coverage. This is the end of the roadmap: a known sampling distribution and the smallest variance any regular estimator can have.

In one sentenceBecause the estimator is asymptotically linear with the efficient influence function, its standard error is the empirical sd of the influence function over root n, and the intervals cover at the nominal rate. The plug-in has neither property.

Geometry follows Schuler & van der Laan, Introduction to Modern Causal Inference, ch. 3–4; the influence-function projection picture is Tsiatis's; the rectangle in scene 7 is the Cauchy–Schwarz bound on the ATE remainder as in Kennedy's notes and Chernozhukov et al. (2018).
