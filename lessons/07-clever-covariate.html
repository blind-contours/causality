From "any direction h" to "the direction we use"

Where the Clever Covariate Comes From

So far h was anything with mean zero, and ϕ told you the slope of ψ along it. Now the question flips: you have an initial fit P̂ that is wrong, and you want to move it. Which direction? This page shows that the answer is h = ϕP̂, the influence function at your current fit, for three reasons that are each a picture. The last picture is the ATE, and the direction there is the clever covariate.

Scene 1

Why ϕ is centered: the uncentered version fails two jobs

Take ϕ(z) = z − ψ and the uncentered candidate ϕ̃(z) = z. Job one: predict how much the mean moves when you add ε of mass at z₀. The true move is ε(z₀ − ψ); ϕ̃ predicts εz₀ and is off by εψ. Job two: measure how far a sample is from a fit. Pₙϕ = (sample mean − fitted mean), which is zero exactly when the fit matches; Pₙϕ̃ = sample mean, which is never a measure of misfit. Both jobs are what the next scenes need.

Mean-zero centering ensures p̂(1+εϕ) integrates to one, but positivity is also required. An unbounded Gaussian score cannot define a nonnegative linear path for every outcome at nonzero ε. Scene 3 instead uses a normalized exponential tilt with finite moment-generating function.

The angle experiment uses the shared normalized finite grid. The Gaussian-mixture tilting experiment below uses exact full-distribution moments; its plotting window does not truncate the statistical model.

Scene 2

Among directions of equal size, ϕ changes ψ the most

Compare directions fairly: fix E[h²] = 1 (same "length" for every h) and ask which one gives the largest slope E[ϕh]. By Cauchy–Schwarz, E[ϕh] ≤ √E[ϕ²]·√E[h²], with equality when h is a multiple of ϕ. So ϕ is the steepest-ascent direction for ψ. Rotate h between ϕ and something orthogonal to it and watch the slope trace a cosine.

max over h with E[h²]=1 of dψ/dε = E[ϕh] is attained at h = ϕ / √E[ϕ²], giving √Var(ϕ)
Among directions h with E[h²] = 1, when is the slope dψ/dε = E[ϕh] largest?
"Least favorable" and "steepest" are the same fact from two sides: the direction in which ψ moves most per unit of likelihood is the direction in which the data are least able to pin ψ down. That's why its Cramér–Rao bound is the largest, and equals Var(ϕ).
In one sentenceThe influence function is the gradient of the parameter, so it's the steepest direction: per unit of movement in the model, ψ changes most along ϕ. That's the direction TMLE fluctuates in.
Scene 3

Move along ϕ by maximum likelihood, and the score equation is Pₙϕ = 0

Start from a wrong fit p̂ (its mean μ̂ is off). Fluctuate along its own influence function with an exponential tilt, p̂ε(z) ∝ p̂(z)·exp(ε·ϕ̂(z)) with ϕ̂(z) = z − μ̂; its score at ε = 0 is ϕ̂ (the linear tilt from earlier pages works the same way but can't take large steps). Choose ε by maximizing the log-likelihood of the sample, Σ log p̂ε(Zi). The derivative of that in ε at ε = 0 is Σ ϕ̂(Zi) = n·Pₙϕ̂. So the likelihood pushes ε in the direction that reduces Pₙϕ̂, and stops where Pₙϕ̂P̂* = 0. That quantity Pₙϕ̂ is the one-step's correction term; TMLE spends it inside the model instead of adding it to the number.

p̂_ε(z) = p̂(z) exp(ε ϕ̂(z)) / c(ε) L(ε) = Σᵢ log p̂_ε(Zᵢ) = ε Σᵢ ϕ̂(Zᵢ) − n log c(ε)
L'(ε) = Σᵢ ϕ̂(Zᵢ) − n·E_{p̂_ε}[ϕ̂] L'(0) = n · Pₙϕ̂ ε̂: Pₙϕ̂ = E_{p̂_ε̂}[ϕ̂] ⇒ mean(p̂_ε̂) = z̄
One-step: μ̂ + Pₙϕ̂ = z̄. TMLE: mean of p̂ε̂ = z̄ as well, because for the mean the score equation says exactly "fitted mean equals sample mean." For nonlinear parameters they differ by a second-order amount.
In one sentenceFor this mean tilt, the score remains the updated mean EIF along the whole path, so fitting ε solves Pₙϕ=0. Matching the initial score alone would not justify the same conclusion for an arbitrary target.

The mean tilt above has a score that equals the updated mean EIF along the path. For a general parameter, matching the EIF only at ε=0 does not guarantee that one likelihood fit solves the updated EIF equation. Iterated locally least-favorable submodels or a valid universal construction may be needed.

Scene 4

For the ATE, the direction on the outcome regression is the clever covariate

Now Z = (X, A, Y). The influence function has two pieces. The X-piece, μ1(X) − μ0(X) − ψ, is already solved by using the empirical distribution of X, so nothing needs to move there. The Y-piece is H(A, X)·(Y − μA(X)) with H = A/π(X) − (1−A)/(1−π(X)). Fluctuating the outcome regression in the direction of its influence function means με(a, x) = μ̂(a, x) + ε·H(a, x). H is the clever covariate. Fit ε by least squares (Gaussian likelihood) and the score equation Σ Hi(Yi − με(Ai, Xi)) = 0 is solved. The regression is pulled hardest where H is large: where the observed arm is rare. That is your pseudopopulation, arriving as a consequence.

ϕ(X,A,Y) = [μ₁(X) − μ₀(X) − ψ] + H(A,X)·(Y − μ_A(X)), H(A,X) = A/π(X) − (1−A)/(1−π(X))
fluctuation: μ_ε(a,x) = μ̂(a,x) + ε·H(a,x) score equation: Σᵢ Hᵢ (Yᵢ − μ_ε(Aᵢ,Xᵢ)) = 0
With a Gaussian outcome the fluctuation is linear and ε̂ = Σ H(Y − μ̂) / Σ H². For binary Y it's on the logit scale, which keeps με in [0, 1]; the score equation is the same.
In one sentenceThe clever covariate is the coefficient of the outcome residual in the efficient influence function. Fluctuating the outcome regression along it, with ε chosen by likelihood, solves the empirical EIF equation for the ATE. The inverse-propensity weights are in it because that's how much each observation's residual moves the parameter.
