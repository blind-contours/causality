From "any direction h" to "the direction we use"

Where the Clever Covariate Comes From

The two-strata lesson found the direction to move the fit by budgeting: spend likelihood where ψ gets the most for it, and the answer was 1/π. This lesson derives the same direction in general. You have an initial fit P̂ that is wrong, and you want to move it. Which direction? The answer is h = ϕP̂, the influence function at your current fit, for three reasons that are each a picture. For the ATE that direction is the clever covariate, and for a binary outcome the move is made on the logit scale, which is TMLE's practical selling point: its estimates stay inside [0, 1] when a one-step correction can leave it.

Scene 1

Why ϕ is centered: the uncentered version fails two jobs

Take ϕ(z) = z − ψ and the uncentered candidate ϕ̃(z) = z. Job one: predict how much the mean moves when you add ε of mass at z₀. The true move is ε(z₀ − ψ); ϕ̃ predicts εz₀ and is off by εψ. Job two: measure how far a sample is from a fit. Pₙϕ = (sample mean − fitted mean), which is zero exactly when the fit matches; Pₙϕ̃ = sample mean, which is never a measure of misfit. Both jobs are what the next scenes need. In practice ψ is unknown, so the figure evaluates the influence function at a fit with mean μ̂: ϕ̂(z) = z − μ̂.

Mean-zero centering ensures p̂(1+εϕ) integrates to one, but positivity is also required. An unbounded Gaussian score cannot define a nonnegative linear path for every outcome at nonzero ε. Scene 3 instead uses a normalized exponential tilt with finite moment-generating function.

The angle experiment uses the shared normalized finite grid. The Gaussian-mixture tilting experiment below uses exact full-distribution moments; its plotting window does not truncate the statistical model.

Scene 2

Among directions of equal size, ϕ changes ψ the most

Compare directions fairly: fix E[h²] = 1 (same "length" for every h) and ask which one gives the largest slope E[ϕh]. By Cauchy–Schwarz, E[ϕh] ≤ √E[ϕ²]·√E[h²], with equality when h is a multiple of ϕ. So ϕ is the steepest-ascent direction for ψ. Rotate h between ϕ and something orthogonal to it and watch the slope trace a cosine.

max over h with E[h²]=1 of dψ/dε = E[ϕh] is attained at h = ϕ / √E[ϕ²], giving √Var(ϕ)
Among directions h with E[h²] = 1, when is the slope dψ/dε = E[ϕh] largest?
"Least favorable" and "steepest" are the same fact from two sides: the direction in which ψ moves most per unit of likelihood is the direction in which the data are least able to pin ψ down. That's why its Cramér–Rao bound is the largest, and equals Var(ϕ).
In one sentenceThe influence function is the gradient of the parameter, so it's the steepest direction: per unit of movement in the model, ψ changes most along ϕ. That's the direction TMLE fluctuates in.
Scene 3

Move along ϕ by maximum likelihood, and the score equation is Pₙϕ = 0

Start from a wrong fit p̂ (its mean μ̂ is off). Fluctuate along its own influence function with an exponential tilt, p̂ε(z) ∝ p̂(z)·exp(ε·ϕ̂(z)) with ϕ̂(z) = z − μ̂; its score at ε = 0 is ϕ̂ (the linear tilt from earlier pages works the same way but can't take large steps). Choose ε by maximizing the log-likelihood of the sample, Σ log p̂ε(Zi). The derivative of that in ε at ε = 0 is Σ ϕ̂(Zi) = n·Pₙϕ̂. So the likelihood pushes ε in the direction that reduces Pₙϕ̂, and stops at the updated fit P̂*, where the influence function recomputed at P̂* has sample mean zero: Pₙϕ* = 0, with ϕ*(z) = z − μ*. That quantity Pₙϕ̂ is the one-step's correction term; TMLE spends it inside the model instead of adding it to the number.

p̂_ε(z) = p̂(z) exp(ε ϕ̂(z)) / c(ε) L(ε) = Σᵢ log p̂_ε(Zᵢ) = ε Σᵢ ϕ̂(Zᵢ) − n log c(ε)
L'(ε) = Σᵢ ϕ̂(Zᵢ) − n·E_{p̂_ε}[ϕ̂] L'(0) = n · Pₙϕ̂ ε̂: Pₙϕ̂ = E_{p̂_ε̂}[ϕ̂] ⇒ mean(p̂_ε̂) = z̄
One-step: μ̂ + Pₙϕ̂ = z̄. TMLE: mean of p̂ε̂ = z̄ as well, because for the mean the score equation says exactly "fitted mean equals sample mean." For nonlinear parameters they differ by a second-order amount.
In one sentenceFor this mean tilt, the score remains the updated mean EIF along the whole path, so fitting ε solves Pₙϕ=0. Matching the initial score alone would not justify the same conclusion for an arbitrary target.

The mean tilt above has a score that equals the updated mean EIF along the path. For a general parameter, matching the EIF only at ε=0 does not guarantee that one likelihood fit solves the updated EIF equation. Iterated locally least-favorable submodels or a valid universal construction may be needed.

Scene 4

For the ATE, the direction on the outcome regression is the clever covariate

Now Z = (X, A, Y). The influence function has two pieces. The X-piece, μ1(X) − μ0(X) − ψ, is already solved by using the empirical distribution of X, so nothing needs to move there. The Y-piece is H(A, X)·(Y − μA(X)) with H = A/π(X) − (1−A)/(1−π(X)). Fluctuating the outcome regression in the direction of its influence function means με(a, x) = μ̂(a, x) + ε·H(a, x). H is the clever covariate. Fit ε by least squares (Gaussian likelihood) and the score equation Σ Hi(Yi − με(Ai, Xi)) = 0 is solved. The regression is pulled hardest where H is large: where the observed arm is rare. That is your pseudopopulation, arriving as a consequence.

ϕ(X,A,Y) = [μ₁(X) − μ₀(X) − ψ] + H(A,X)·(Y − μ_A(X)), H(A,X) = A/π(X) − (1−A)/(1−π(X))
fluctuation: μ_ε(a,x) = μ̂(a,x) + ε·H(a,x) score equation: Σᵢ Hᵢ (Yᵢ − μ_ε(Aᵢ,Xᵢ)) = 0
With a Gaussian outcome the fluctuation is linear and ε̂ = Σ H(Y − μ̂) / Σ H². For binary Y it's on the logit scale, which keeps με in [0, 1]; the score equation is the same. Scene 5 shows why that matters.
In one sentenceThe clever covariate is the coefficient of the outcome residual in the efficient influence function. Fluctuating the outcome regression along it, with ε chosen by likelihood, solves the empirical EIF equation for the ATE. The inverse-propensity weights are in it because that's how much each observation's residual moves the parameter.

The clever covariate H(A, X) is the weight in the AIPW correction with the arm's sign attached: A/ĝ(X) − (1−A)/(1−ĝ(X)). Below, on the course cohort, each residual stick is drawn as thick as its |H|, and the correction is Pₙ[H·(Y − m̂A(X))], exactly the sum the fluctuation above drives to zero. Hover a coloured term to find it in the figure. With stratum means as the outcome model the correction is already zero, which is why TMLE would not move at all: ε̂ = 0.

Scene 5

For a binary outcome, fluctuate on the logit scale and stay inside [0, 1]

Now Y is an event (1 = yes). Eight patients, an initial outcome model Q̂(a, x) = P(Y = 1 | A = a, X = x), and a propensity π(x). Patients 7 and 8 share a covariate value where treatment is rare, and patient 7 was treated anyway and had the event that the model thought unlikely (Q̂ = 0.30). The one-step estimator adds the mean of H·(Y − Q̂) to the plug-in, and H = 1/π for patient 7 is large. Nothing stops that sum from carrying an estimated risk above 1.

TMLE moves the model instead: logit Q*(1, x) = logit Q̂(1, x) + ε₁/π(x) for the treated regression and logit Q*(0, x) = logit Q̂(0, x) + ε₀/(1 − π(x)) for the control one (the two pieces of H, one ε each), with ε chosen by logistic maximum likelihood. Every Q* is an expit, so every Q* is a probability, and so is their mean.

logit Q*(a, x) = logit Q̂(a, x) + ε_a · H_a(x), H₁ = 1/π, H₀ = 1/(1 − π) score: Σ_{A=a} H_a (Y − Q*) = 0
ψ* = (1/n) Σᵢ [Q*(1, Xᵢ) − Q*(0, Xᵢ)] one-step = ψ̂ + (1/n) Σᵢ H(Aᵢ, Xᵢ)(Yᵢ − Q̂(Aᵢ, Xᵢ))
With π = 0.10 for patient 7, the one-step estimate of the treated risk E[Y(1)] is 1.41, an impossible probability. What will the logistic TMLE give?
Slide π(x₇) up to 0.5 and the one-step comes back inside [0, 1]: the problem is the large weight 1/π on a surprising outcome, which is exactly the near-positivity situation where a bounded estimator is worth having.

This example has a finite logistic fit in each arm. Completely separated samples (every treated Y equal to 1, say) put the optimum at ε = ±∞; they need separate diagnostics.

In one sentenceThe logistic fluctuation solves the same score equation as the linear one, but every updated prediction is a probability, so the TMLE of a risk is a risk and the TMLE of a risk difference lies in [−1, 1]; the one-step estimator adds an unbounded correction and can leave the parameter space when weights are large.
